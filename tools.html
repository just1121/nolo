What happens if you don’t use RO?

  • Off FlavorsHeat contact during distillation can leave a cooked, altered character in the finished wine.

    Heat Damage Leaves a Chemical Fingerprint

    Heat exposure doesn’t just dull a wine’s flavor — it changes its chemistry in measurable, predictable ways. Both red and white wines develop elevated furfural and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) — sugar-dehydration byproducts — along with faster loss of free sulfur dioxide (SO2) and increased benzaldehyde once exposed to elevated temperature (AJEV).

    White wines show it mainly in the aromatics: the fruity acetate esters isoamyl acetate, hexyl acetate, and 2-phenylethyl acetate break down quickly, while 1,1,6-trimethyl-1,2-dihydronaphthalene (TDN) and vitispirane — compounds normally associated with years of bottle age — appear prematurely, alongside faster browning.

    Red wines show it mainly in color and structure: monomeric anthocyanins decline while tannin-anthocyanin polymeric pigments build up, pushing color toward a more stable but duller, browner hue and coarsening tannin texture (AJEV).

    These markers are exactly what a targeted lab panel can detect — giving winemakers objective, chemical evidence of heat exposure rather than relying on taste alone.

  • Lost AromaVolatile aromatics are stripped out and must be added back—and the round trip is never perfect.

    Reverse osmosis keeps flavor and aroma compounds where they belong: in the wine. The difference comes down to whether those compounds ever leave the wine in the first place. The membrane’s pore size is small enough to let only water and ethanol pass through, while phenolics, tannins, pigments, and the vast majority of flavor and aroma molecules — simply too large to fit through the membrane — stay behind in the wine itself. RO doesn’t extract these compounds and reintroduce them later — they were never removed to begin with.

    Distillation-based methods work the opposite way. Vacuum distillation and spinning cone technology both lower alcohol’s boiling point to evaporate it out of the wine — but ethanol isn’t the only thing that vaporizes at those temperatures. Volatile esters, terpenes, and other aroma compounds share similar volatility and vaporize right alongside the alcohol and water. To compensate, these methods have to try to recapture that vaporized aroma fraction separately — in a trap or a dedicated “flavor strip” pass — and then blend it back into the dealcoholized wine afterward.

    Once aroma compounds are stripped out, isolated, and stored separately, recovery is never complete.

  • Lost VolumeDistillation pulls water out with the alcohol—a fully dealcoholized lot can come back up to a quarter smaller.
  • Lost BodyStripped-and-rebuilt wines read thinner: less sweetness, less roundness, lower scores in blind tastings.
